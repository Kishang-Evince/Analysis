# How designers can shape AI behavior through context

The rise of AI in enterprise environments has fundamentally shifted how designers approach user experience, moving beyond traditional interface design to shape the very behavior of intelligent systems. Rather than simply crafting [prompts](https://www.glean.com/blog/what-is-an-ai-prompt) or commands, today's designers must create contextual frameworks that guide AI to understand not just what users ask, but what they truly need within their specific work environment.

Context has emerged as the critical bridge between human intention and AI capability, transforming static interactions into dynamic, adaptive experiences. When AI systems understand the full scope of a user's situation-their role, current task, organizational knowledge, and workflow patterns-they can deliver responses that feel intuitive rather than mechanical.

This evolution demands a new design paradigm: one where UX flows become the primary mechanism for teaching AI how to behave appropriately across diverse enterprise scenarios. By embedding contextual understanding into every interaction point, designers can create AI systems that adapt to users' needs rather than forcing users to adapt to the technology.

## Context in AI design

Context in AI design represents the foundational layer that transforms generic AI capabilities into tailored enterprise solutions. At its core, context encompasses the rich tapestry of information surrounding each user interaction: the user's department, their current project phase, historical patterns of information access, team dynamics, and even temporal factors like deadlines or quarterly cycles. This multidimensional understanding enables AI to move beyond surface-level responses to deliver genuinely helpful insights.

The implementation of contextual AI design relies heavily on sophisticated data structures like [knowledge graphs](https://www.glean.com/blog/knowledge-graph-agentic-engine), which map relationships between people, documents, projects, and processes across an organization. These graphs enable multi-hop reasoning-allowing AI to connect disparate pieces of information that might seem unrelated but hold critical relevance for a specific user's query. For instance, when a sales representative asks about a client proposal, the AI can traverse the knowledge graph to understand not just the proposal document itself, but also recent email exchanges, similar successful proposals from the team, relevant product updates, and even the client's interaction history.

### Building contextual intelligence

Creating truly contextual AI requires three fundamental components: While Google achieves [95%](https://slite.com/en/learn/enterprise-search-survey-findings) first-page accuracy, internal enterprise searches succeed only 10% of the time. Employees spend an average of 1.8 hours per day searching for relevant knowledge, losing 166.4 hours annually per employee to redundant searching.

-   **Environmental awareness**: The AI must understand the user's immediate work environment, including the applications they're using, the documents they have open, and their recent activities. This real-time context helps the AI provide responses that align with the user's current focus.
    
-   **Historical patterns**: By analyzing past interactions and behaviors, AI systems can anticipate needs and preferences. This includes understanding communication styles, frequently accessed resources, and typical workflow sequences that vary by role and individual.
    
-   **Organizational knowledge**: The AI needs access to the collective intelligence of the enterprise-from formal documentation and policies to informal knowledge captured in conversations and collaborative work. This broader context ensures responses align with company standards and leverage institutional wisdom.
    

### The role of agentic reasoning

Agentic reasoning represents the next evolution in contextual AI design, where systems don't just respond to queries but actively reason through complex scenarios. This capability allows AI to decompose intricate business problems into manageable steps, considering context at each decision point. For example, when an HR professional needs to create a new employee onboarding plan, an agentic AI system can break this down into subtasks: reviewing current policies, identifying role-specific requirements, checking compliance regulations, and suggesting a timeline based on past successful onboardings. Companies that excel in three key AI adoption areas are [32 times](https://www.ibm.com/thought-leadership/institute-business-value/en-us/report/agentic-ai-operating-model) more likely to achieve top-tier business performance than those with minimal implementation. 95% of employees in organizations with the highest levels of agentic AI use report improved job satisfaction.

The power of [agentic reasoning](https://www.glean.com/blog/a-complete-guide-to-agentic-reasoning) lies in its ability to maintain context across these multiple steps, ensuring each action builds coherently toward the ultimate goal. This approach mirrors how experienced professionals tackle complex tasks-by understanding the full context, breaking down the problem, and systematically working through solutions while adapting to new information along the way.

## Why designers need to focus on UX flows

Designers are essential in crafting experiences where AI systems respond intelligently within user workflows. Instead of relying solely on static prompts, designers create dynamic flows that adapt to specific user contexts. This method ensures AI operations remain intuitive and contextually relevant across various scenarios.

### The importance of structured workflows

Structured [workflows](https://www.glean.com/product/agent-orchestration) enhance AI's ability to deliver tailored interactions by providing a coherent framework for decision-making. Through comprehensive user journey mapping, designers can foresee potential user needs and integrate solutions seamlessly. This foresight allows AI to act in alignment with user expectations, rather than reacting unpredictably.

-   **Reliability**: Crafted workflows ensure AI interactions are predictable, fostering user confidence and satisfaction.
-   **Streamlining**: By defining common pathways, AI minimizes redundant tasks, enabling users to allocate time to strategic endeavors.

### Adapting to user contexts

While maintaining structure, workflows must also accommodate individual user scenarios. This balance enables AI to adjust its responses dynamically, guided by user-specific data and real-time interactions.

-   **Customization**: Designers can build adaptability into workflows, allowing AI to modify its actions according to individual preferences and historical interactions.
-   **Versatility**: Flexible workflows allow AI to effectively serve diverse user groups, adapting as organizational needs and contexts evolve.

### Seamless ecosystem integration

For AI to function effectively, it must integrate smoothly into existing processes. Designers focus on creating AI interactions that complement and enhance user activities without causing disruption, ensuring AI tools fit within the broader organizational framework.

-   **Cross-department collaboration**: By engaging with various stakeholders, designers align AI initiatives with overarching business objectives and user requirements.
-   **Comprehensive experience design**: Considering the full scope of the user journey allows designers to create cohesive systems that highlight AI's strengths, ensuring they add value without overwhelming users.

By centering on UX flows, designers empower AI systems to deliver experiences that anticipate user needs and adapt to future challenges, ensuring a robust and user-centric AI environment.

## How to shape AI behavior

Shaping AI behavior starts with embedding fundamental UX principles into every interaction. By crafting seamless interfaces and workflows, AI systems can effectively predict and address user needs, ensuring responses are both relevant and timely.

### Leveraging UX principles

Designers must prioritize simplicity and clarity to create intuitive experiences. Clear communication minimizes errors and enhances user satisfaction.

-   **Consistency**: Maintaining uniform design elements across platforms ensures users can navigate AI systems with ease, reducing cognitive strain.
-   **Feedback**: Providing immediate responses and confirmations helps guide users, reinforcing correct actions and preventing mistakes.

### Understanding the user's path

Designers are pivotal in ensuring AI interactions are not only efficient but empathetic, seamlessly integrating technology into everyday workflows. However, long-term AI use correlates significantly with [mental exhaustion](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12367725/) (r = 0.905) and inversely correlates with users' self-confidence in independent decision-making (r = −0.360), suggesting extended AI interaction may diminish long-term cognitive autonomy despite short-term efficiency gains.

-   **Path mapping**: By illustrating user journeys, designers pinpoint opportunities for AI to intervene, ensuring interventions are both timely and pertinent.
-   **Empathy**: Recognizing user emotions and needs enables AI to offer solutions that resonate personally, fostering stronger connections.

### Implementing adaptive reasoning

Adaptive reasoning empowers AI to address complex tasks by breaking them into manageable actions, dynamically adjusting to user needs.

-   **Action sequencing**: Dividing larger tasks into smaller, actionable items allows AI to handle complexity without overwhelming users.
-   **Flexibility**: Learning from interactions, AI systems continuously refine their responses, enhancing their ability to cater to diverse user requirements.

Designers are pivotal in ensuring AI interactions are not only efficient but empathetic, seamlessly integrating technology into everyday workflows.

Designing AI with a focus on context means embedding situational awareness into every user interaction. By understanding the nuances of each user's environment, AI systems can transition from mere tools to adaptive partners that respond intelligently to evolving needs.

### Customizing AI engagements

Customizing AI engagements involves deeply analyzing user-specific scenarios to ensure interactions meet individual expectations.

-   **Real-time adjustment**: AI must continuously evolve its responses based on user behavior and preferences, ensuring relevance.
-   **Targeted solutions**: By accessing data on user roles and historical interactions, AI can deliver precise and timely solutions.

### Utilizing knowledge graphs

Knowledge graphs are essential for enhancing AI's understanding of organizational dynamics. These structures allow AI to draw meaningful connections across various data points.

-   **In-depth connectivity**: Knowledge graphs provide a network of insights, enabling AI to address complex inquiries with depth.
-   **Proactive responsiveness**: With a clear grasp of interdependencies, AI systems can offer well-informed and timely responses.

Contextual design empowers AI to not only interact but anticipate, seamlessly integrating into workflows and enhancing productivity.

## Step 2: develop UX patterns for AI

Developing UX patterns for AI involves establishing frameworks that shape AI behavior, ensuring interactions remain intuitive and user-centric. These patterns act as guidelines, providing a structured approach to handling diverse situations while maintaining the flexibility to adapt instantly. By focusing on designing AI behavior, designers can ensure that AI systems meet user expectations by delivering seamless interactions.

### Creating consistent interaction models

Consistent interaction models are crucial for guiding AI behavior in a way that feels reliable and coherent. By defining clear interaction pathways, AI systems can maintain predictability across various user scenarios.

-   **Foundation for engagement**: These models establish a baseline for AI responses, ensuring coherence throughout different interactions.
-   **Embedded logic**: By embedding logical pathways, AI can manage routine tasks efficiently while adapting to specific user needs.

### Designing flexible workflows

Flexibility within workflows is key to ensuring AI remains responsive and relevant. This adaptability allows AI systems to adjust behavior based on immediate user inputs, enhancing overall usefulness.

-   **Contextual modifications**: UX patterns include strategies for AI to adjust actions contextually, based on evolving user needs, ensuring responses are both timely and accurate.
-   **Proactive responsiveness**: By harnessing contextual data, AI systems can refine their interactions, offering solutions that align with user expectations.

Empathetic design begins with a deep exploration of user behaviors and expectations. This insight allows AI systems to respond in ways that genuinely address user goals. Empathetic design begins with a deep exploration of user behaviors and expectations. This insight allows AI systems to respond in ways that genuinely address user goals.

-   **Holistic insights**: Analyzing diverse user interactions highlights challenges and preferences, enabling AI to provide valuable solutions.

### Personalizing AI responses

Tailoring AI interactions to individual preferences ensures each user finds the AI responsive and relevant.

Empathy bridges technology and human experience, ensuring AI systems understand and cater to nuanced user requirements.
